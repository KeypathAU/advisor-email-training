Write So They Write Back

Your students are tired, busy, and reading on a phone. Here is what goes in an email, what to check before you send, and why any of it works.

01

Who you are actually writing to

Four things that are true about the person on the other end. Get these and most of the writing sorts itself out.

The reader

Four things that are true

Where they are

On a phone, standing up

Between a shift and dinner. Anything longer than a screen and a half gets pushed to later, and later usually means never.

What they are juggling

Study is the fourth thing in their day

You are not competing with their other subjects. You are competing with work, kids and being worn out.

Why they go quiet

Going quiet is shame, not rudeness

Anything you ask for is something they can avoid, and the further behind they are the more attractive avoiding it gets.

What they cannot hear

They cannot hear your tone

Neutral wording reads as cool, and cool reads as disappointed. You have to put the warmth in on purpose.

The avoidance spiral

The student who most needs you is the least able to answer you.

A week behind is awkward to admit. A month behind is humiliating. Every week of silence puts up the price of replying, so the people in the most trouble get harder to reach the longer it runs.

This is why chasing harder makes it worse. You are not applying pressure. You are raising a price they already cannot pay.

The way out is to bring the price down. Make the reply small, make a bad answer an acceptable one, and take the explaining off them.

02

What goes in an email

Five parts, in this order. People read top to bottom and stop the moment it stops being worth the effort. Each part has one job.

Structure

Five parts, one job each

1

Subject line. Make the promise.

2

First line. Get to the point.

3

The middle. Only what they need.

4

The ask. One thing, clearly.

5

The close. Leave the door open.

If a part is not doing its job, cut it.

1

Subject line

Job: make the promise

It answers two questions before they open anything. What is this about, and can it wait?

On a phone they see about the first forty characters. Put the part that has to survive at the front and let the tail get cut.

Not this

Following up

This

Getting your Week 4 assessment sorted

2

First line

Job: get to the point

If they read one line and close the email, that line has to be enough.

Not this

I hope this email finds you well. I am writing to follow up on my previous correspondence regarding your progress.

This

Your Week 4 assessment hasn't come through. I wanted to write myself rather than let the system chase you.

3

The middle

Job: only what they need

Every extra sentence is a tax on your ask. Background you are adding so they have the full picture is background you are adding for yourself.

Try it: delete a sentence. If they would still do the right thing, leave it deleted.

Read this bit twice

Short is not the same as kind.

Cutting means removing the words that do no work. It does not mean removing the words that do the human work, and those are always the first to go when someone is being efficient.

A student cannot tell the difference between an advisor who is busy and an advisor who is annoyed. If you cut until only the facts are left, what arrives at their end is not efficiency. It is disapproval.

4

The ask

Job: one thing, clearly

One email, one ask. Two asks gets you the easy one. Three gets you nothing.

Not this

Please let me know your thoughts, and also make sure your contact details are up to date.

This

Could you reply with one line telling me where you're up to? Anything is useful, including "haven't started".

5

The close

Job: leave the door open

Two lines. One that treats them like a person instead of a record, and one that makes replying feel safe. Spend your warmth here.

This

No need to write anything long, and no need to explain yourself. I'll take it from there and we'll work around your shifts.

03

Three checks before you send

About twenty seconds all up. Do them in this order and you will catch nearly everything.

The ask from this huddle

Run these on every email

Check one

The squint

Look at it on a phone without reading a word. Can you see where the ask is? If it is not obvious at a glance, anyone skimming will miss it. Everyone is skimming.

Check two

The flat voice

Read it out loud, flat and impatient. Email always lands colder than you meant it, so the flat reading is closer to what they get than yours is.

Check three

The count

Count the decisions you are asking for, then take one away. "Let me know what suits" is three decisions. "Tuesday 10am or Thursday 2pm?" is one.

04

Why any of this makes a difference

Five things happening in the head of the person reading. This is what lets you handle situations nobody has written a template for.

Processing fluency

They judge the effort before they read a word.

People decide whether to deal with an email based on how it looks, not what it says. That is why 200 words in one block gets pushed to later and the same 200 words in five short paragraphs gets answered. White space is a signal that says this will be quick. Formatting is doing persuasion work.

Negativity bias

Warm gets read first, and email strips the warm out.

People work out whether you are friendly before they work out whether you are any good, and they do it in the first line or two. Written text carries none of the signals that normally do that job, so a neutral email is not received as neutral. It is received as cool.

Decision friction

Every extra option costs you a reply.

Two named times beat "whenever suits you", because "whenever" makes them open a calendar, weigh it up and commit, and each of those is somewhere to give up. A second ask does not add half as much work again. It adds a decision about which one to do first, and that is where people stall.

Self-determination

Push and they go quiet. Hand them the wheel and they move.

Adults switch off when they feel controlled and re-engage when they feel in charge. "You need to submit by Friday" invites silence. "The deadline's Friday, and if that's not going to work, tell me and we'll look at options" names the same deadline but leaves them in control. You are not softening the message.

Self-efficacy

People move when the next step looks small.

A student who is behind is not weighing up your deadline. They are weighing up whether they can face the whole subject, and your email is the thing making them think about it. Shrink the step until it is smaller than the dread. "Send me your first paragraph" gets a reply that "submit your assessment" never will.

05

What it looks like in practice

The same email twice, the six emails that do not land, and the words to drop.

Worked example

The same email, twice

Both are accurate. Both are professional. Only one gets a reply.

Before
Subject: Following up

Hi Sarah,

I hope this email finds you well. As per our records, you have not yet submitted the Week 4 assessment which was due on 8 August, and you have also not completed the Week 3 discussion post. Unfortunately, failure to submit assessments can impact your progression. Please advise as to your intentions at your earliest convenience.

After
Subject: Getting your Week 4 assessment sorted

Hi Sarah,

Your Week 4 assessment hasn't come through. I wanted to write myself rather than let the system chase you.

Week 4 is where a lot of people hit a wall, especially on shift work. It's fixable.

Could you reply with one line telling me where you're up to? Even "haven't started" is useful.

Worked example

What changed and why

01

The subject names an outcome, not a chase. "Following up" sounds like trouble, which is what someone avoiding you will not open.

02

The reason is in sentence one. If she reads nothing else, she knows what this is.

03

The second ask is gone. The discussion post goes in its own email, another day.

04

The consequences are held back. Progression is real, but leading with it triggers avoidance in exactly the student who most needs to reply.

05

The ask shrank to something she can do badly. "Haven't started" is an acceptable answer, and that is the whole trick.

06

"Week 4 is where a lot of people hit a wall." One sentence saying this is normal, and it does more for the reply rate than any amount of politeness.

Diagnostics

Six emails that do not land

The Wall

All true, none of it readable. One block, no ask visible. Put off before it is read.

The Buried Ask

The thing you need is halfway through paragraph three. Your last line is "kind regards", not the ask.

The Bundle

Three requests to save yourself a send. You get the easiest one, or none. The word "also" turns up.

The Over-Apology

So much softening the message disappears. "Sorry to bother you, just wondering if maybe."

The Robot

Correct, complete, nobody home. Nothing in it could only have been written to this student.

The Leak

Frustration coming through as formality. "As previously advised." "As I've already explained."

Tone

How warm, how firm

Most of your inbox sits in the first two. Firm is somewhere you move on purpose, after a warmer go has been ignored, and never because you are annoyed.

Default for problems

Warm

"Week 4 catches a lot of people. Tell me where you're up to and we'll sort it out."

Default for the day

Direct

"Your enrolment's confirmed for Term 3. Classes open on 14 October."

Last resort

Firm

"If we don't hear from you by Friday your enrolment will be withdrawn. I'd rather that didn't happen, so reply even if it's just to say you need more time."

Before you hit send

Seven questions. If any answer is no, it is not finished.

Does the subject line say what this is and whether it can wait?
Is the reason I wrote in the first sentence?
Is there exactly one ask?
Is the ask on its own line, with a date on it?
Can I delete a sentence without changing what they'd do?
Would this feel warm to someone having a rough week?
Is the next step small enough that someone who's done nothing can still reply today?
That is the whole thing
