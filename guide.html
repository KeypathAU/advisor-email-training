Advisor email

Nobody reads your email. They scan it and decide.

Your students are tired, busy, and reading on a phone. Here's how to write something they'll actually answer: what goes in an email, what to check before you send, and why any of it works.

For anyone at Keypath writing to online students
The reader

Who you're actually writing to

Everything here comes from four things that are true about the person on the other end. Get these and most of the writing sorts itself out.

Where they are

They're on a phone, standing up

Between a shift and dinner, on a screen the width of your hand. Anything longer than a screen and a half gets pushed to later, and later usually means never.

What they're juggling

Study is the fourth thing in their day

You're not competing with their other subjects. You're competing with work, kids and being worn out. Assume there's nothing spare and write for that.

Why they go quiet

Going quiet is shame, not rudeness

Anything you ask for is something they can avoid, and the further behind they are, the more attractive avoiding it gets. Silence is rarely a decision. It is a flinch.

What they can't hear

They can't hear your tone

They can't tell that you're being friendly. Neutral wording reads as cool, and cool reads as disappointed. You have to put the warmth in on purpose.

The avoidance spiral

The student who most needs you is the least able to answer you.

A week behind is awkward to admit. A month behind is humiliating. Every week of silence puts up the price of replying, because there is more to explain and more to be embarrassed about. So the people in the most trouble get harder to reach the longer it runs, and they are the ones you can least afford to lose.

This is why chasing harder makes it worse. Every unanswered email adds to what they will have to account for when they finally do answer, so each one you send makes the next reply less likely, not more. You are not applying pressure. You are raising a price they already cannot pay.

The way out is to bring the price down. Make the reply small, make a bad answer an acceptable one, and take the explaining off them entirely. You are not lowering your standards. You are removing the reason to stay silent.

Structure

What goes in an email, in this order

The order matters because people read top to bottom and stop the moment it stops being worth the effort. Each part has one job. If a part isn't doing its job, cut it.

1

Subject line

Job: make the promise

It answers two questions before they've opened anything. What's this about, and can it wait? Name the actual thing, plus the action or the date. Six to nine words. If your subject line could sit on top of any email you've ever sent, it isn't a subject line.

Not this

Following up / Checking in / Quick question / Important information

This

Your Week 4 assessment is due this Friday

2

First line

Job: get to the point

Your first sentence carries the reason you wrote. Not the greeting, not the history, not the context. If they read one line and close the email, that line has to be enough for them to know what's going on.

Not this

I hope this email finds you well. I'm writing to follow up on my previous correspondence regarding your progress in the course.

This

Your Week 4 assessment hasn't come through yet, and I'd like to help you get it in.

3

The middle

Job: only what they need

Give them what they need to do the right thing, and nothing else. Every extra sentence is a tax on your ask. Background you're adding so they've "got the full picture" is background you're adding for yourself.

Try it: delete a sentence. If they'd still do the right thing, leave it deleted.

4

The ask

Job: one thing, clearly

One email, one ask. Two asks gets you the easy one. Three gets you nothing. Make it specific, put a date on it, and make it small enough that someone who's done nothing can still answer today.

Not this

Please let me know your thoughts and if there's anything else you need, and also make sure your contact details are up to date.

This

Can you reply by Thursday with one line telling me where you're up to? Even "haven't started" is useful.

5

The close

Job: leave the door open

Two lines. One that treats them like a person instead of a record, and one that makes replying feel safe. Spend your warmth here, because it's the last thing they read and it's what they carry into whether they answer.

This

You're not behind in a way we can't fix. Reply whenever suits and I'll take it from there.

Checks

Three things to check before you send

About twenty seconds all up. Do them in this order and you'll catch nearly everything.

The squint

Look at it the way they will. On a phone, without reading a word. Can you see where the ask is? If it isn't obvious at a glance, sitting on its own line, anyone skimming will miss it. Everyone is skimming.

Squint at it.
Where does your eye land?

The flat voice

Read it out loud in the flattest, most impatient voice you can manage. Email always lands colder than you meant it, so the flat reading is closer to what they'll get than yours is. If it survives that, it's safe to send.

Read it like you're fed up.
Does it still hold?

The count

Count what you're asking them to do. Decisions to make, links to open, documents to dig up, things to remember. Then take one away. "Let me know what suits" is three decisions. "Tuesday 10am or Thursday 2pm?" is one.

Count the decisions.
Remove one.
Why it works

Why any of this makes a difference

None of the above is style preference. Here's what's actually happening in the head of the person reading, which is also what lets you handle situations nobody's written a template for.

Processing fluency

They judge the effort before they read a word

People decide whether to deal with an email based on how it looks, not what it says. That's why 200 words in one block gets pushed to later and the same 200 words in five short paragraphs gets answered. White space isn't decoration. It's a signal that says "this'll be quick". Formatting is doing persuasion work.

Negativity bias

Warm gets read first, and email strips the warm out

People work out whether you're friendly before they work out whether you're any good, and they do it in the first line or two. Written text carries none of the signals that normally do that job, so a neutral email isn't received as neutral, it's received as cool. Unless you add warmth deliberately, the default reading is that you're unimpressed.

Decision friction

Every extra option costs you a reply

Two named times beat "whenever suits you", because "whenever" makes them open a calendar, weigh it up and commit, and each of those is somewhere to give up. Same with a second ask. It doesn't add half as much work again, it adds a decision about which one to do first, and that's where people stall.

Self-determination

Push and they go quiet. Hand them the wheel and they move.

Adults switch off when they feel controlled and re-engage when they feel in charge. "You need to submit by Friday" invites silence. "The deadline's Friday, and if that's not going to work, tell me and we'll look at options" names the same deadline but leaves them in control of the response. You're not softening the message. The deadline is still there.

Self-efficacy

People move when the next step looks small

A student who's behind isn't weighing up your deadline. They're weighing up whether they can face the whole subject, and your email is the thing making them think about it. Shrink the step until it's smaller than the dread. "Send me your first paragraph" gets a reply that "submit your assessment" never will, and once they've answered once, the next one is far easier.

Example

The same email, twice

Both are accurate. Both are professional. Only one of them gets a reply.

Before
To: s.mitchell@student.edu.au
Subject: Following up

Hi Sarah,

I hope this email finds you well. I am writing to follow up on my previous email of 12 August regarding your progress in the course. As per our records, you have not yet submitted the Week 4 assessment which was due on 8 August, and you have also not completed the Week 3 discussion post. Unfortunately, failure to submit assessments can impact your progression and may affect your eligibility to continue. Please advise as to your intentions at your earliest convenience. Additionally, please ensure your contact details are up to date in the student portal.

Kind regards,

After
To: s.mitchell@student.edu.au
Subject: Getting your Week 4 assessment sorted

Hi Sarah,

Your Week 4 assessment hasn't come through yet, and I'd like to help you get it in.

Week 4 is where a lot of people hit a wall, so you're in good company, and it's fixable.

Can you reply with one line telling me where you're up to? Even "haven't started" is useful. I'll take it from there and we'll work out something that fits around your job.

No need to write anything long.

Six things changed

  • The subject line names an outcome, not a chase. "Getting it sorted" sounds like help. "Following up" sounds like trouble, which is exactly what someone avoiding you won't open.
  • The reason is in sentence one. If she reads nothing else, she knows what this is about.
  • The second ask is gone. Contact details go in their own email, another day. Bundling them halved the odds of getting either.
  • The consequences are held back. Progression and eligibility are real, but leading with them triggers avoidance in exactly the student who most needs to reply. Save it for the firm version, after a warm one has gone unanswered.
  • The ask shrank to something she can do badly. "Haven't started" is an acceptable answer, and that's the whole trick. It removes the reason to stay silent.
  • "Week 4 is where a lot of people hit a wall." One sentence that says this is normal, and it does more for the reply rate than any amount of politeness.
What goes wrong

Six emails that don't land

Once these have names, you'll start spotting them in your own drafts before you send.

The Wall

All true, none of it readable. One block of text, no breaks, no visible ask. It gets put off before it gets read.

You can tell it's bad from across the room.

The Buried Ask

The thing you need is halfway through paragraph three. They read the first line and the last line and miss it completely.

Your last line is "kind regards", not the ask.

The Bundle

Three requests in one email to save yourself a send. You get the easiest one, or none, then chase the other two anyway.

The word "also" turns up.

The Over-Apology

So much softening the message disappears. It reads as unsure, so they're unsure it needs doing at all.

"Sorry to bother you, just wondering if maybe."

The Robot

Correct, complete, and nobody home. It reads automated, so it gets treated as automated, which means ignored.

Nothing in it could only have been written to this student.

The Leak

Your frustration coming through as formality. They hear the irritation and stop taking in anything else.

"As previously advised." "As I've already explained."
Tone

How warm, how firm, and when to shift

Most of your inbox sits in the first two. Firm is somewhere you move on purpose, after a warmer go has already been ignored, and never because you're annoyed. If you're annoyed, write it and send it tomorrow.

Warm

First time you raise a problem, anyone who's gone quiet, anyone you think is struggling. Assumes good faith out loud.

"Week 4 catches a lot of people. Tell me where you're up to and we'll sort it out."

Direct

Routine business. Deadlines, confirmations, anything factual. Friendly but quick. This is your default for most of the day.

"Your enrolment's confirmed for Term 3. Classes open on 14 October and you'll get access on the day."

Firm

A real consequence is close and the warm approach has already been tried. Say the consequence plainly, once, and still leave a way out.

"If we don't hear from you by Friday your enrolment will be withdrawn. I'd rather that didn't happen, so reply even if it's just to say you need more time."
Words

Words to drop

Not because they're wrong, but because they're doing the opposite of what you think. Most of them are habits picked up from corporate email, and they put distance between you and the student.

Drop this Say this
Just checking in Your assessment's due Friday and I haven't seen a draft yet
As per my previous email In case my last one got buried, here's the short version
Please advise Can you tell me which of these two works?
Unfortunately we are unable to Here's what we can do instead

Before you hit send

Seven questions. If any answer is no, it isn't finished.

  1. Does the subject line say what this is and whether it can wait?
  2. Is the reason I wrote in the first sentence?
  3. Is there exactly one ask?
  4. Is the ask on its own line, with a date on it?
  5. Can I delete a sentence without changing what they'd do?
  6. Would this feel warm to someone having a rough week?
  7. Is the next step small enough that someone who's done nothing can still reply today?
